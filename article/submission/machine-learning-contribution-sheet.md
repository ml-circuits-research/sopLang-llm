# Machine Learning contribution information sheet

Manuscript: Taught operations, taught wording: what small models transfer when fine-tuned to write programs

## 1. Main claim and significance

Small open models of 0.5B to 1.7B parameters, fine-tuned to translate word problems into executable programs, transfer what their curriculum covers in the wording it covers. Unseen compositions of taught operations were solved completely in the training generator's wording. Rewording degraded trained and held-out compositions alike. Book problems whose solution shape had been taught were solved once reworded into the teaching template, even where the same run solved none in the book's wording. Learning rate, adapter, data mixture, target structure, prompt, and model size in the tested range did not produce transfer to untaught shapes. Untuned 1.5B and 1.7B models writing plain JavaScript with one example composed the elementary operations nearly as well as the fine-tuned models, and the untuned 1.7B model solved most problems of a type that its fine-tuned versions almost never solved; the same untuned model writing the new language solved none, so that loss is not attributable to fine-tuning alone. Two larger prompted models solved nearly everything, so these limits are not properties of the task.

The significance is practical. Curricula for small program-writing models should cover wording as deliberately as operations, and results should be reported against the untuned model writing plain code, which is a strong baseline and, outside the curriculum, can be stronger than the fine-tuned model.

## 2. Evidence

Results are percentages of the stated number of problems; the samples were sized for qualitative contrasts (the manuscript states their resolution in Section 2.3).
- **Coverage ladder (0.5B, first series):** 99% of 323 validation examples with trained programs, 13% of 16 with untrained programs, and none of 225 held-out problems from untrained types.
- **Composition inventory:** 100% of 160 problems from four unseen compositions of taught operations, in every inventory run. With a newly added operation, results were partial and unstable across data versions (60% to 100% of 160). Outside the inventory they stayed below 1%.
- **Rewording test (semantic level, the same problems at every wording):** the 0.5B run solved 75%, 17% and 21% of 24 held-out-composition problems in the original, paraphrased, and narrative wording, and 100%, 56% and 67% of 9 trained controls; the 1.7B run solved 100%, 55% and 73% of 33, and 100%, 75% and 92% of 12.
- **Book problems in teaching wording:** three 1.7B runs taught the shapes solved all 45 rewritten scheduling problems (15 each); one of them solved none of the same 15 in the book's wording. A run never taught the shapes solved none in either wording.
- **Fine-tuned against prompted bases, on the same 115 problems:**
  - held-out compositions: untuned models writing JavaScript solved 60%, 89% and 82% of 55 at the three sizes, and the fine-tuned students 82%, 100% and 100%;
  - book types: the fine-tuned 1.7B model solved 58% of 55, and no untuned condition exceeded 24%.
- **Withheld type, all 40 problems:** the untuned 1.7B model writing JavaScript solved 60%; the fine-tuned 1.7B runs solved 5% and none; the untuned 1.7B model writing SOP Lang from the prompt solved none, so the loss is not attributable to fine-tuning alone.
- **Prompt bytes:** restoring a statement's final newline, which a first re-run had dropped, reproduced the original programs in 58 of 60 coalition problems (38 of 60 without it).
- **Larger models, same prompts, no fine-tuning:** Claude Haiku 4.5 and Claude Sonnet 5 solved 100% of the 55 held-out compositions writing JavaScript or SOP Lang, 85% to 98% of the 55 book problems, all 40 withheld-type problems, and 94% to 100% of 33 problems in both generator and narrative wording. Direct answering separated them: Haiku 87% and 75%, Sonnet 100% and 96%.
- **Measurement:** every saved program was re-executed, and every outcome reproduced. The evaluation-only comparisons are scored by two blind judges (κ = 0.97), with calibration items in the passes that judged the small models.

## 3. Closest contributions and relation

Lake and Baroni (PMLR 80, 2018) and Kim and Linzen (EMNLP 2020) show systematic generalization failures on purpose-built benchmarks. Keysers and colleagues (ICLR 2020) show that failures track compound divergence while atoms stay familiar; our small models, by contrast, transferred to unseen compounds of familiar operations, but only in the training wording. Shaw and colleagues (ACL 2021) separate compositional splits from natural language variation; our rewording test measures both on the same problems, and the fine-tuned models handled the first and not the second. Work on wording robustness in math word problems (Patel and colleagues, NAACL 2021; Shi and colleagues, ICML 2023; Mirzadeh and colleagues, ICLR 2025) concerns prompted models; our setting fixes the training wording, so the distance from it can be set on purpose. Dziri and colleagues (NeurIPS 2023) describe subgraph matching in transformers; our failure anatomy resembles it at the level of whole programs. Shi and colleagues (DL4C 2022; ICLR 2024) show poor compositional generalization in neural program synthesis; our inventory result shows complete transfer within a generator for a fine-tuned small model. Bogin and colleagues (EMNLP 2022) find unobserved local structures hard; our unobserved structure is usually one operation. Fu and colleagues (ICML 2023) describe specialization trade-offs in small models; we observe the same capability loss and show that it moves the capability into the compiled path for taught plans. Olausson and colleagues (ICLR 2024) find self-repair limited by diagnosis; our retries, tested only on problems within the curriculum, recovered execution but not correctness.

## 4. Limitations stated in the paper

The runs used one seed per condition, and the untrained-program validation column has 16 items. The central positive result is confounded with rendering by the training generator, and the data version that introduced the inventory also removed repeated scaffolding. The held-out set was reused adaptively, so it is a development benchmark. The operators are synthetic and elementary. The scale comparison covers one model family. The statements and references of two held-out books were edited during the programme, and each run was scored on the items current at its evaluation. Single greedy decodes were sensitive to one byte of prompt layout. The semantic level rests on two judges of the same model family as the larger models. The larger models were evaluated through agent sessions rather than independent API calls, and they may have seen similar problems in pretraining.
